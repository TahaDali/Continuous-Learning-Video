# Storytelling framework

Use when constructing a sustained explanation, realistic work scene, or continuation of a learning series. The learner is an adult who may be new to the subject.

The default first response is an organization chapter, so its main characters should help explain why the institution exists and how it works. Do not rush immediately to an intern or employee completing an assignment. That work scene becomes richer once the learner understands the institution, its stakeholders, and its decisions.

## Give the explanation a causal spine

Choose a believable customer, employee, or operational situation that exposes why the business needs the role. A story earns its space when the learner sees how one event creates the next requirement.

For example, an advertiser wants to reach decision makers. Sales proposes a campaign. Delivery needs dates and approved materials. Finance needs consistent commercial records. Management needs a forecast. Each need introduces the next team or system naturally.

Do not force every organization into a sale. A hospital appointment, public-service application, internal software rollout, or bank's management report may be a better case for the role.

Introduce the fictional setting once, clearly: “The people, dialogue, amounts, and internal workflow in the following case are fictional teaching examples.” Renew the label when introducing a new simulation, table of results, or scene that could be mistaken for a public company fact. Do not attribute invented quotes to real named executives.

## Build a scene that teaches judgment

Use the following shape flexibly, without turning every paragraph into a formula:

1. **Situation:** A person needs an outcome under a real constraint.
2. **Tension:** Information is incomplete, departments disagree, a system fails, or two measures appear inconsistent.
3. **Investigation:** Show what the employee checks and why the order matters.
4. **Choice:** Explain plausible options and the relevant tradeoff.
5. **Consequence:** Show the customer, colleague, financial, or operational effect.
6. **Evidence:** Explain how anyone would know the intervention helped.

Let the employee sometimes discover that the first explanation was wrong. Competence includes changing a hypothesis when evidence disagrees. Avoid writing fictional success as automatic or portraying one department as foolish merely to make the hero look capable.

Keep dialogue short and purposeful. A manager's question should reveal a decision; a colleague's objection should reveal a constraint. Explain unfamiliar terms in the sentence where they become necessary, then use the professional term consistently.

Sustain the case across the chapter: introduce a need, reveal why one solution is insufficient, explain what the organization adds, and show the consequences for different stakeholders. A fictional opening followed by unrelated lists, or one isolated work anecdote in a general job guide, does not by itself reproduce the intended teaching experience.

## Example: trace one extra placement

**Fictional scene from Learning Press.** A commitment specifies four newsletter
placements, while an operational report counts five. The analyst first asks what
that report measures and how it identifies records. A superseded proposal is one
possible explanation; a duplicated delivery receipt is another.

Keep the same commitment visible as evidence arrives. Inspect revision identity,
report filters and the approved schedule before choosing a correction. Show which
observation rejects an alternative. Agree on the business definition, test both
the repaired case and an unaffected case, and explain what is still unknown.

The lesson is the investigation and the consequence of a decision, not an
impressive-looking improvement number. See [the worked case](teaching-case.md).

## Use analogies briefly

An analogy should explain a relationship and then hand the reader back to the real process. A CRM can resemble a shared customer notebook because teams need a common history. It also has access rules, automation, and linked records, so the notebook metaphor stops being sufficient.

Prefer one useful analogy per difficult idea. Avoid opening every chapter with a different airport, restaurant, bridge, or hospital. Explain where an analogy breaks if that limit could mislead. Do not make simplified examples distort accounting, security, regulated responsibilities, or system behavior.

## Develop capability at three levels

| Level | What the learner should be able to do | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Understand | Explain purpose and relationships in ordinary language | Explain why a sales forecast and cash balance can differ |
| Apply | Work through a bounded problem and justify checks | Reconcile conflicting report totals using definitions and records |
| Master | Handle ambiguity and design for other people and future cases | Agree on shared metrics, ownership, exception handling, and monitoring across teams |

Add a small exercise or worked choice when it helps the user prepare to perform the work. Explain what evidence a credible answer would contain. Do not assign lengthy homework by default or imply that reading a worked example establishes professional competence.

When contrasting employee responses, avoid caricatures. An adequate analyst may resolve one assigned record correctly. A strong analyst may identify and validate a recurring cause. An exceptional analyst may also coordinate a proportionate prevention measure and demonstrate its effect. Escalation or asking for help may be the best decision at any level.

## Preserve a continuing world

Keep fictional names, roles, transaction amounts, dates, and measures consistent across parts. If an amount changes, explain the event: an initial $200,000 proposal can become a $150,000 agreement. Do not silently switch customers or rewrite a previously established fact.

Maintain a compact continuity note in the accessible conversation or user-requested saved notes:

- Subject and target role; intended audience and depth.
- Completed part and next requested part.
- Fictional cast, transaction, and important established definitions.
- Current sources and facts that need rechecking.
- Unresolved problem or decision that leads into the next part.

Do not write to global memory, modify the installed skill, or claim permanent cross-chat recollection merely because a series continues. The reusable skill stores the method; the current conversation or supplied notes store this learner's story.

Begin a continuation with only the recap needed to understand the next decision. End with the question the next part will resolve when a series was requested. If the user asked for a complete analysis, finish the analysis instead of withholding chapters behind repeated invitations.
